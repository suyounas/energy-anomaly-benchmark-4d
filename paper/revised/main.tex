\begin{abstract}
That no anomaly detector is best everywhere is well established in general time-series benchmarks. What an energy practitioner cannot tell from those benchmarks is \emph{why} rankings change and how much each design choice contributes. We examine this for energy demand and price series with documented events (GB and Texas demand, commodity prices, a simulated European grid). First we show how an earlier four-dataset comparison produced an apparent duration--resolution rule from a mixed event-/timestamp-level F1, an uncontrolled alert budget and confounded variables; the rule does not survive a single evaluation contract, and a held-out test on 862 labelled public series from 23 sources does not recover it (partial Spearman $\rho=-0.05$, 95\% interval $[-0.16, 0.14]$). Second, a fully crossed semi-synthetic design (648 datasets, 9 detectors, 2 feature sets) attributes the variance of detector skill: the detector main effect carries 19.9\% and detector $\times$ setting interactions about 26\%, so rankings are conditional on event type, duration and magnitude. Third, on real GB and ERCOT data with equal-budget tuning and paired block-bootstrap intervals, simple baselines (a seasonal-naive residual, a matrix profile) match or beat LSTM and Transformer autoencoders, the winner depends on the event, and two events per series cannot rank detectors. We specify the protocol (inputs, labels, thresholds, metrics), test label validity directly, and release code, tests and every result file.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Choosing an anomaly detector for energy time series is a practical problem, and the general benchmarks (TSB-UAD, TSB-AD, TimeEval) already report that no detector wins everywhere and that simple methods are often competitive. The open question for an energy analyst is more specific: given \emph{this} kind of event on \emph{this} kind of series, which design choices---detector family, input features, event type and duration, sampling resolution, evaluation metric---actually move the result, and by how much? Answering it requires holding the pipeline fixed while one thing changes, and real energy series with documented events (one or two per series) cannot do that alone.

The starting point is an earlier comparison of five detectors on four datasets that reported a selection rule and a ``detection gap'' tied to how many native samples an event spans. A re-examination identified problems in its evidence: mixed metrics, confounded variables, one or two events per dataset, unequal tuning, an under-specified protocol, unexamined label validity and missing related work. This paper reports what remains after correcting them. We propose no new detector. The contributions are empirical and methodological:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A specified protocol} (Sec.~\ref{sec:protocol}): exactly what the models see, how dated events become labels, how alerts are formed and how each metric is computed, with event-level and timestamp-level metrics never mixed.
\item \textbf{Direct tests of label validity} (Sec.~\ref{sec:labels}): whether the series deviates inside labelled events, how many unlabelled days deviate as much, and whether detector rankings survive plausible label errors.
\item \textbf{Attribution, not just ranking}: a fully crossed semi-synthetic design (Sec.~\ref{sec:factorial}) that separates event type, duration, magnitude, resolution, background, feature set and detector, and a held-out test of the earlier rule on 862 public series (Sec.~\ref{sec:public}).
\item \textbf{A real-data comparison with stronger baselines and uncertainty} (Sec.~\ref{sec:real}): equal-budget tuning, simple and distance-based baselines from the benchmark literature, paired block-bootstrap intervals, and per-event coverage.
\item Released code with tests, including regression tests for bugs found during this work (event end day, window seams, a wrong VUS-PR bootstrap).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
Several benchmark and evaluation studies are directly relevant. TSB-UAD~\cite{paparrizos2022tsb} collects 13{,}766 labelled univariate series and evaluates twelve detectors; TSB-AD~\cite{liu2024elephant} curates 1{,}070 series from 40 datasets, benchmarks statistical, neural and foundation-model detectors (33 are listed in its repository), recommends VUS-PR as the most reliable measure and reports that simpler methods often outperform advanced neural networks. Schmidl et al.~\cite{schmidl2022comprehensive} re-implemented 71 detectors and evaluated them on 976 series, finding no single best algorithm and that deep methods were not competitive with simpler ones; their toolkit is~\cite{wenig2022timeeval}. Wu and Keogh~\cite{wu2023flawed} document triviality, unrealistic anomaly density, mislabelled ground truth and run-to-failure bias in common benchmarks. Kim et al.~\cite{kim2022rigorous} show that point-adjusted F1 can make a random score look state-of-the-art, and Paparrizos et al.~\cite{paparrizos2022vus} argue for threshold-independent range-aware measures. The survey of Darban et al.~\cite{darban2024survey} and its companion list of deep methods, datasets and evaluation metrics (\url{https://github.com/zamanzadeh/ts-anomaly-benchmark}) catalogue about fifty benchmark datasets and ten evaluation metrics and give guidance on when to use each.

\paragraph{Positioning.} These works establish, across hundreds to thousands of generic series, that rankings are dataset-dependent and that simple baselines are strong. We therefore do \emph{not} claim ``no universal detector'' as a finding. What they do not provide, and what this paper adds for energy data, is (i) a \emph{decomposition} of how much of the variation in detector skill is due to the detector, the event type, its duration and magnitude, the sampling resolution and the input features, measured on a fully crossed design where those factors are independent; (ii) a protocol written down at the level needed to reproduce an energy study with dated external events; (iii) direct tests of whether such event labels are valid; and (iv) an account of how a plausible-looking rule emerged from confounded variables and failed a held-out test. Energy series differ from the generic benchmarks in having strong multi-scale seasonality and labels that are \emph{documented external events} (storms, a pandemic) rather than annotated points, which is why the label-validity question is not secondary here.

\section{Label Provenance and Validity}\label{sec:labels}
A detector that flags an unlabelled period cannot show that the period is a true event, so a label set cannot be validated by reporting that detectors ``find'' events the labels miss. We therefore test validity without using detectors where possible.

\paragraph{Provenance and tiers.} Tier~1 events are documented crises fixed before modelling (Texas Freeze, Winter Storm Elliott, the 2020 lockdown, the December 2022 cold snap, the two market events). Tier~2 labels are post hoc: for ERCOT, a period of all-time demand records between 27 June and 10 August 2023~\cite{ercot2023records} was identified \emph{after} seeing detector output and is used only as a labelled sensitivity analysis, never in headline results. Year-on-year demand changes recorded in the data records show that labels differ greatly in how visibly they affect the series: GB lockdown $-19.1\%$, ERCOT Elliott $+49.4\%$, ERCOT Texas Freeze $+30.9\%$ (with load-shedding caveats), but the GB cold snap only $+6.4\%$, a weak label that we treat as such.

\paragraph{(A) Does the series deviate inside labelled events?} For each event we compute the causal seasonal-naive residual (value minus the median of the same time of week over the previous four weeks, scaled by its robust spread) and report the mean daily deviation inside the event, the share of its timestamps above $|z|>3$, and the percentage of non-event days with a smaller deviation. This is independent of every detector. All four labelled events depart from the seasonal expectation (Table~\ref{tab:labelA}). The two ERCOT events and the GB cold snap are clear: their mean daily deviation lies above 95\% of non-event days and 24--51\% of their timestamps exceed $|z|>3$, against 2--3\% elsewhere. The GB lockdown is the weakest label: mean daily deviation 1.27, above 80\% of non-event days, 9\% of timestamps beyond $|z|>3$. This is expected for a demand level that stayed low for ten weeks, because the four-week baseline adapts after the first weeks, so the measure understates it; we report the number as computed and do not tune it.

\paragraph{(B) How incomplete are the labels?} We count days outside every labelled event whose daily deviation is at least that of the weakest labelled event. These are \emph{candidates} for missing labels, not claimed anomalies: holidays and weather can produce them. Reporting the count, rather than detector flags, quantifies label incompleteness without circularity. In GB, 546 of 2{,}842 days outside the events (19\%) reach the weakest event's level (1.26), a lenient bar because the lockdown is a weak label; 61 of them lie between 24 December and 2 January, calendar effects that a four-week baseline does not model. In ERCOT, 69 of 1{,}816 days (3.8\%) reach 2.46. Three of them (11--13 February 2021, daily deviation 2.7, 4.1 and 4.7) immediately precede the labelled start of the Texas Freeze on 14 February, which suggests that the labelled window starts late relative to the series' own departure; 31 January to 2 February 2023 (4.4, 4.3, 3.5) is a further strong, unlabelled departure. We have not checked these dates against operator records or weather data. The labels are therefore incomplete by this measure: the metrics treat every such day as normal, so alerts on them count as false positives.

\paragraph{(C) Do rankings survive label errors?} Detector scores are computed once; only the labels change: event starts or ends shifted by 1, 3 or 7 days (shifts at least as long as the event are skipped), both boundaries moved outward by the same amounts (dilation), events lengthened or shortened by half, and each event dropped in turn. We report whether the best detector and the full ranking (Kendall $\tau$) are unchanged (Table~\ref{tab:labelC}); detectors use default configurations and three seeds, and the label sets are not independent, so the shares are descriptive.

\textbf{GB.} Boundary errors of up to seven days, dilation, shortening and lengthening leave the matrix profile best in all 18 such label sets (Kendall $\tau\ge0.90$ for AUC-PR, $\ge0.88$ for timestamp F1). The one exception is dropping the lockdown, after which seasonal naive is best ($\tau=-0.24$ and $-0.05$). That label is 1{,}679 of the 1{,}919 labelled timestamps (87\%); without it prevalence falls from 3.4\% to 0.44\% and every detector except seasonal naive scores AUC-PR between 0.003 and 0.004, close to prevalence. The GB ranking is therefore a statement about the lockdown label, not about boundary precision. \textbf{ERCOT.} Rankings are less stable. The best detector by AUC-PR changes in 2 of 17 label sets (dropping Winter Storm Elliott, and moving the Texas Freeze start three days later; both make the matrix profile best), with $\tau$ down to 0.62; by timestamp F1 it changes in 9 of 17 (47\% unchanged), with $\tau$ down to 0.49. The top two, seasonal naive and matrix profile, swap under day-level changes, consistent with two events being too few to rank detectors (Sec.~\ref{sec:real}).

\paragraph{Limits.} Documented external events are a coarser label than point annotations: the labelled window covers days in which many individual timestamps look normal, which is why timestamp-level recall is bounded and why per-event coverage is reported. Public benchmark labels have their own flaws~\cite{wu2023flawed}; Sec.~\ref{sec:public} therefore treats them as noisy and reports sensitivity subsets.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
\subsection{Pipeline}
(1)~\emph{Resolution.} A series is used at its native resolution unless stated; aggregation to a coarser resolution takes the mean over the block. (2)~\emph{Features.} Each timestamp has a four-feature vector (percentage return, rolling mean, rolling standard deviation and rolling skew of the return, window 30 days of time in the equal-budget runs and 30 rows in the published pipeline); the \emph{residual} variant adds a causal seasonal-naive residual (above). Scalers are fitted on training rows only. (3)~\emph{Split.} Either a forward 70/30 split (``A'') or a clean-window split (``B''): training on a stretch with no labelled event, testing on the rest. On real series nothing is injected into the test split; the synthetic designs inject events only into the test part and keep training clean. (4)~\emph{Fit and score.} The neural models are trained on the windows of the training rows (window 30 rows) and score a test row by the reconstruction error of the window ending there; classical detectors score rows (or, for the ``window'' variants, the same windows). All detectors in the equal-budget runs are \emph{inductive}: every reference quantity comes from training rows. (5)~\emph{Alerts.} A fixed alert budget flags the top 5\% of test scores. This uses the test score distribution (a quantile of the test scores) and is therefore an \emph{alert-budget} evaluation, not a deployable threshold; DBSCAN as published flags its noise set and has no budget (its alert rate floats; Sec.~\ref{sec:sweep}). (6)~\emph{Labels to metrics.} Each timestamp inherits the label of the event window that contains it. All detectors are evaluated on the \emph{same} rows (the intersection of the rows every model can score).

\subsection{Detectors and settings}
Table~\ref{tab:det} lists the settings of the five original detectors. The equal-budget runs add, with the same 5\% budget and the same features: a seasonal-naive residual score ($|$residual$|$), the one-line first-difference z-score of Wu and Keogh~\cite{wu2023flawed}, PCA, $k$-NN and isolation forest on the same 30-step windows the neural models see, the matrix profile~\cite{yeh2016matrix} (z-normalised nearest-neighbour distance to the clean subsequences, computed here with a matrix product, and checked against a brute-force reference in the tests), and the spectral residual~\cite{ren2019spectral}. Each family is given the default configuration plus 8 random configurations; selection uses a validation split of the clean training window into which synthetic events (level shifts, variance bursts, ramps and pulses of several durations) are injected, so real test events never influence a choice; a family is only tuned if validation can separate its configurations, otherwise its default is kept.

\subsection{Metrics}
\textbf{Timestamp-level} precision, recall and F1 count timestamps. \textbf{Event-level} metrics count events and alerts: an alert is a maximal run of flags, an event is detected if any flag falls within its window widened by a tolerance (one day in the equal-budget runs), and precision is the fraction of alerts that overlap an event window. The two are never combined: the earlier Table~I paired timestamp-level precision with event-level recall, which is not an F1 of anything. \textbf{Per-event coverage} is the share of an event's timestamps that carry a flag. \textbf{AUC-PR} (average precision; chance level $=$ prevalence $\pi$) is threshold-free, and we report \textbf{VUS-PR}~\cite{paparrizos2022vus} point values from our own re-implementation (cross-checking against the official package is listed in Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}). Because a fixed budget $b=5\%$ caps timestamp F1 at $\F^{\max}=2\min(\pi,b)/(\pi+b)$ ($0.75$ at $\pi=3\%$), we report $\pi$ and $\F^{\max}$ with every result. \textbf{Uncertainty:} seeds do not capture the uncertainty from having few events, so we use a paired block bootstrap (7-day blocks, the same resampled rows for every detector, 200 replicates), report $\Delta$ to the best detector, and mark a detector ``reliably worse'' only if the interval of the difference excludes zero. The best detector is chosen post hoc, so such statements are exploratory. For range metrics a plain block bootstrap is invalid (it breaks events and lets buffers cross seams); we use event-preserving resampling units (Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}).

\section{Controlled Duration--Resolution Sweep}\label{sec:sweep}
\paragraph{Design.} We generate 9.6 years of 30-min data (daily, weekly, annual seasonality; AR(1) residual; $t_6$ noise), clean for the first 60\% (train) and with injected events in the final 40\% (test). Event \emph{duration} takes 2\,h, 12\,h, 1\,d, 3\,d, 7\,d, 14\,d. The number of events is chosen so that anomalous prevalence is constant at $\pi\approx3\%$ (realised range 0.0299--0.0317), with at least three events in every cell. Events are either \emph{level shifts} (+3 standard deviations) or zero-mean \emph{variance bursts} (3 s.d.), start on day boundaries, and never overlap. The \emph{same} series is analysed at 30\,min, 1\,h and 1\,d (mean aggregation; cells where an event is shorter than one sample are omitted). Ten seeds vary noise and event placement. Detectors: Isolation Forest, DBSCAN as published, an inductive kNN-to-train distance, the $|$return$|$ top-5\% score (what Studies~2--4 call rolling $z$), a true rolling $z>3$, and a PCA window-reconstruction model (window 30 samples) standing in for a windowed autoencoder. The PCA surrogate is \emph{not} the LSTM-AE or Transformer; neural models are not run here \pending{run neural models in the sweep on GPU}. Rolling features use a window fixed at 7 days of time.

\paragraph{Result 1: the best detector depends on duration relative to the detector's time scale.} For level shifts (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}, top), the rolling $z$ is clearly best up to 1\,d (AUC-PR 0.89, 0.97, 0.71) consistent with its 7-day trailing baseline not yet having adapted to the shift; from 3\,d it collapses (0.33 at 3\,d, 0.10 at 7\,d), consistent with the baseline absorbing the event, and Isolation Forest is best (0.59, 0.78; a tie with kNN at 14\,d). A window-30 PCA model peaks at 12\,h--1\,d (0.38, 0.34), consistent with the event filling its 15-hour window (mechanisms here are inferred, not separately tested). For variance bursts the picture is different: a per-point $|$return$|$ rule is best up to 3\,d, and windowed or learned models catch up (statistical ties) at 7--14\,d. The ordering therefore changes with event type as well as duration, which the cross-dataset comparison could not separate.

\paragraph{Result 2: resolution alone does not reproduce the claimed DBSCAN decline.} At fixed duration, DBSCAN's F1 changes by at most 0.021 (shifts) and 0.022 (bursts) between 30\,min and 1\,h, a doubling of sample count. Against duration, DBSCAN's F1 at 30\,min is U-shaped, not monotone (shift: 0.35, 0.24, 0.26, 0.32, 0.35, 0.32 from 2\,h to 14\,d), and its AUC-PR is nearly flat (0.14--0.20). The earlier claim that DBSCAN falls monotonically with event sample count is not reproduced when prevalence, series and domain are held fixed.

\paragraph{Result 3: DBSCAN's alert rate floats, and for level shifts it dominates its F1.} DBSCAN's realised alert rate is 11--24\% for shifts and 9--21\% for bursts, against 5\% for the other detectors (Fig.~\ref{fig:alert}). Across runs, its F1 correlates $-0.92$ with that alert rate for level shifts but $+0.10$ for bursts, so the artefact is event-type dependent. Cross-dataset DBSCAN F1 values from a transductive, floating-budget detector should not be read as detection quality.

\paragraph{Result 4: thresholded F1 and AUC-PR can disagree completely.} The true rolling $z>3$ flags at most 2.0\% of points in any cell (mean over seeds), and at daily resolution its F1 is exactly 0 in every cell, while its AUC-PR is 0.97 at the 1-day event (Fig.~\ref{fig:f1}). A fixed threshold, not the score, determined the F1.

\section{Attribution on a Fully Crossed Semi-Synthetic Design}\label{sec:factorial}
Real series cannot separate event type, duration, magnitude, resolution, background and feature set, because each real series brings one value of each. We therefore cross them. \textbf{Design} (pre-registered before the run): 3 backgrounds (strongly seasonal; weakly seasonal and noisy; strongly seasonal with trend) $\times$ 3 event types (level shift, variance burst, ramp) $\times$ 3 durations (4, 24, 120\,h) $\times$ 3 magnitudes (3, 6, 12 residual standard deviations at the peak) $\times$ 2 resolutions (1\,h, 4\,h) $\times$ 4 replicates $=648$ datasets, each with 120 clean training days and 90 test days containing three events. Nine detectors with default configurations, under both feature sets, are scored on identical rows (nine detectors $\times$ two feature sets = 11{,}664 results). The outcome is normalised AUC-PR (skill) $=(\mathrm{AP}-\pi)/(1-\pi)$, because lift is bounded by $1/\pi$ and $\pi$ varies with duration. A sum-of-squares decomposition of the balanced design gives each factor's share of the variance (Table~\ref{tab:factorial}); detectors that do not read the feature columns return identical scores under both feature sets (a structural zero).

\paragraph{Result.} The detector main effect explains only 19.9\% of the variance; everything that involves the detector (main effect plus its two-way interactions) explains 47.4\%, so more than half of detector-related variance is conditional on the setting. The interactions with duration, event type, magnitude and resolution sum to about 26\%, more than the main effect itself. Under the rule fixed in advance this means that statements of the form ``A beats B'' are not portable across settings and must be stated per setting. Rankings are only moderately stable: the detector with the highest mean skill (a seasonal-naive residual) is best in 56\% of 324 conditions, and eight of nine detectors win at least one. Event type is decisive: the seasonal-naive residual wins 76\% of shift and 81\% of ramp conditions but only 11\% of burst conditions, where the first-difference one-liner wins 44\%.
\paragraph{Caveat.} The synthetic backgrounds are exactly seasonal with additive noise, the situation for which a seasonal-naive residual is built; its global win says little about real grids, and the shares describe this design and its level choices, not energy data in general. The feature-set effect is small here (0.5\%) because only four of nine detectors read the features; on real data it was larger (Sec.~\ref{sec:real}).

\section{Held-Out Test of the Withdrawn Rule on Public Series}\label{sec:public}
\paragraph{Pre-registered test.} The earlier rule said that reconstruction models gain over density models as events span more samples. We tested it on the univariate TSB-AD series~\cite{liu2024elephant}: for each series, the training prefix named in the file name, a period estimated by autocorrelation, nine detectors scored on the same test rows, and AUC-PR per detector. Window PCA (reconstruction) and $k$-NN-to-train (density) stand in for the two model classes, because the neural models are too slow for 870 series. The primary statistic is the partial Spearman correlation between log median event length (samples) and the AUC-PR difference, controlling for log prevalence, computed within each source dataset, combined by Fisher $z$ over datasets with at least eight series, with a bootstrap over datasets. The decision rule (supported / contradicted / not supported by the 95\% interval) was committed before any benchmark series was analysed.

\paragraph{Result.} 862 series from 23 source datasets (17 with at least eight series; 8 of 870 files excluded: 7 with no anomaly in the test part, 1 detector failure): $\rho=-0.047$, 95\% interval $[-0.163, 0.139]$, which contains zero, so the rule is \textbf{not supported}. Sensitivity subsets give the same answer (Table~\ref{tab:pub}). Per-dataset estimates disagree in sign. Window PCA beats $k$-NN on only 25\% of series (median AUC-PR difference -0.094), so the stand-in pair is a weak proxy for the original comparison; the test therefore says that event length does not predict this advantage, not that no reconstruction model could show one. A first run that omitted 40 series because of a bug in our period estimator (flat training prefix) gave $\rho=-0.060$ with the same verdict; we report both.

\section{Discussion: a Checklist}
The results suggest that an energy anomaly benchmark should report: (1) event-level and timestamp-level metrics separately; (2) prevalence, realised alert rate and $\F^{\max}$; (3) a threshold-free metric next to any fixed-budget F1; (4) inductive fitting, or explicit disclosure of transductive fits; (5) simple baselines (a seasonal-naive residual, a one-line difference score) and the input features given to every model; (6) per-event results and intervals that account for the number of events, with no ranking from one or two events; (7) the label provenance, and tests of label validity that do not rely on detectors; (8) independent variation of duration, resolution and event type when claiming a duration effect, and constant prevalence when varying duration.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}
\item Two events per real series cannot rank detectors; the real-data comparison is evidence about \emph{which things matter}, not about a winner. Documented operator-declared event lists, not used here, could raise the count.
\item All detectors are univariate; relations between series (the 4{,}097 grid buses, other balancing areas) are not used.
\item Fixed-budget flags use a quantile of the test scores; this is an alert-budget evaluation, not a deployable threshold.
\item The factorial and sweep designs are synthetic; their backgrounds favour seasonal-naive scores; level choices were taken from the real-data runs.
\item The neural models are not in the factorial or public-benchmark tests (cost), so the public test uses window PCA and $k$-NN as stand-ins. Stronger recent detectors (foundation models, Series2Graph, NORMA, TranAD) are not evaluated.
\item VUS-PR is our re-implementation; agreement with the official package is not yet measured \pending{run \texttt{experiments/crosscheck\_official.py}}. VUS-PR bootstrap intervals from the runs of 2 Nov 2026 used an invalid resampling and are not reported; the fixed procedure exists but the runs have not been repeated \pending{repeat the tuning runs and report VUS-PR intervals}.
\item The published neural numbers of the earlier pipeline (Table~\ref{tab:real}) build windows across the gap of the clean-window split (about 30 windows) and were not regenerated; the equal-budget runs use segment-aware windows.
\item Label validity (Sec.~\ref{sec:labels}) is assessed on four events in two series. Analysis~A and B use a seasonal-naive residual, so they are blind to anomalies that the baseline absorbs; candidate days in B were not checked against operator records; the perturbations in C are a stress test, not a model of how real annotation errors arise.
\item DBSCAN is not in the equal-budget runs (transductive, no budget); the sweep (Sec.~\ref{sec:sweep}) covers it.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
A rule that looked well supported by four datasets was the product of mixed metrics, floating alert budgets and variables that moved together; it fails a controlled sweep and a held-out test on 862 public series. What remains is more modest and more useful: detector rankings on energy data are conditional on event type, duration, magnitude and input features, simple baselines are competitive with deep autoencoders, and a handful of events cannot establish a winner. The protocol, the label-validity analyses and the released tests are meant to let others measure those dependences on their own series rather than assume them.
